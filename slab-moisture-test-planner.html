The rules behind it

How the Plan Is Worked Out

How many tests

ASTM F2170 calls for three test locations for the first 1,000 sq ft (100 m²) and one more for each additional 1,000 sq ft. A 10,000 sq ft floor needs at least twelve. The planner rounds partial areas up — a 1,500 sq ft room gets four — because testing more is always allowed and testing less is not.

Where to put them

On slabs on grade or below grade, place at least one location within 3 ft (0.9 m) of each exterior wall, where moisture conditions near the foundation often differ from the middle of the floor. If you have more exterior walls than the area minimum allows for, add locations until each wall is covered.

How deep to drill

Probe depth depends on how the slab dries. A slab drying only from the top — on grade, below grade, or over a vapour retarder — is tested at 40% of its thickness. A suspended slab drying from both faces is tested at 20%. A typical 4 in. basement slab therefore needs holes about 1.6 in. deep.

When a reading counts

The space has to be at service conditions for 48 hours before testing begins — enclosed, with HVAC holding the temperature and humidity it will have in use — and those conditions have to hold through the test. Probes then need 24 hours to equilibrate before the reading is recorded. You will still see 72 hours quoted in older guidance; that changed with the 2018 revision of the standard.

What the reading has to be

F2170 is a test method. It sets no pass mark of its own. The acceptable reading comes from the flooring and adhesive manufacturers’ data sheets — use the lower of the two. Conventional adhesives are often limited to around 75–85% RH, while moisture-tolerant systems go considerably higher. That is why this planner asks you for the limit rather than assuming one.

Meters are not acceptance tests

Non-destructive surface meters under ASTM F2659 are useful for mapping which parts of a slab are relatively wetter, and for choosing where to drill. They don’t produce readings you can accept a floor on.

Flatness

ASTM F710, the practice for preparing concrete to receive resilient flooring, uses a flatness figure of 3/16 in. in 10 ft. Many flooring manufacturers state their own tolerance, and the current edition of F710 is the one your contract names — so the record lets you change the figure.

This planner summarises ASTM F2170 and F710 as they are commonly applied. It does not reproduce the standards. If your contract references them, buy the current editions from ASTM.
